How to choose between three options when you like them all

28 October 2026 · 5 min read

Two options make a choice. Four make a list, and the difference is visible. Three is the most awkward number: they all look roughly equally good, which is exactly why the decision stalls for days.

Why three in particular

With two options any advantage decides it: a bit cheaper, a bit closer, a bit more interesting. With four or more, one usually drops out on its own — too expensive, too far, too complicated. Three often come out equal in “overall appeal”, because you have to compare them on several criteria at once.

Hence the main idea: stalling is not a sign that you are indecisive. It is a sign that the criterion has not been chosen yet. As soon as there is a criterion, the three become two.

Technique 1. Ask which one you would definitely not pick

The question “which is better?” makes you defend everything at once. The question “which one would I definitely not choose?” is answered in a second. Remove it and two options remain, and ordinary comparison works on them.

Technique 2. Compare in pairs

Take the first two and decide which is better. Compare the winner with the third. This way you never hold three parameters in your head at once — the brain is bad at that. Pairwise comparison always produces a result, even if the difference is tiny.

Technique 3. Change the level of the question

“Where should we go” is a question about options. “How much am I willing to spend and how far am I willing to travel” is a question about the criterion. The second is almost always easier to answer. Once the criterion is named, the unsuitable options drop out by themselves, without an inner fight.

It helps to write down one main constraint for each option: price, travel time, the need to prepare. Often one of the three loses on a constraint rather than on appeal.

Technique 4. Limit the stakes

If all three are good, the cost of a mistake is small. That is an important conclusion: you can choose quickly and almost any of them. Realising “this decision will not ruin my life” removes half the tension.

Technique 5. Use the draw as a reaction test

Spin a wheel with three options. Then watch not the wheel but yourself: if it lands on B and you feel a pang of disappointment, then what you actually wanted was C. Here the draw does not decide, it reveals.

This is not psychology on faith: the reaction to a specific result is often more honest than ten minutes of deliberation. But there is a condition — the result has to be taken seriously, at least for a few seconds. If you immediately think “fine, that does not count”, the test will not work.

If the draw leaves you disappointed, do not spin again. Use it as the answer: take the option you regretted losing. A second spin is needed in exactly one case — if you could not read your own reaction at all.

What to avoid

  • Do not add a fourth option “just in case” — it only takes you back to the start.
  • Do not try to take everything at once: two out of three is also a decision, but usually the worst one in terms of time and effort.
  • Do not ask five people for advice — you will get five criteria and zero clarity.
